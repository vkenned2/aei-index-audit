## Abstract

Statistics describing the geographic spread of artificial intelligence are entering policy
discussion faster than their measurement properties are being characterized, and increasingly they
carry decision rules: a region is above or below proportional use, a jurisdiction is in the top tier,
a gap is or is not closing. We show that analytical choices which are typically undisclosed can alter both the estimated values
and the classifications drawn from them.

Using the Anthropic Economic Index across five waves and 51 United States jurisdictions, we apply a
simple threshold rule and vary only the configuration. Holding the wave fixed and varying only the geography, between 33% and 49% of states change
classification depending on which of five official United States government maps is used, with a mean
of 39% across waves. Across the full grid of five maps by five waves, 67% of states are unstable. Decomposing the uncertainty in the headline inequality statistic, the standard deviation across
those five maps is 14.4 times the sampling standard deviation, and the two intervals do not overlap.
Holding resolution fixed at nine regions so that scale plays no part, variation across random
contiguous partitions is 15.2 times the sampling variation at the same resolution. Choosing the map moves the published figure
further than collecting the data does.

Three properties of the index construction explain this. It is areal: aggregating states into
Census divisions removes 41 to 74% of measured inequality depending on weighting convention, and
against a null of random contiguous nine-region partitions the reporting map falls at the 78th to
95th percentile, high in the distribution generated by our partition ensemble at that resolution. It is
**spatially structured**: usage exhibits significant positive autocorrelation (Moran's I = 0.278,
p = 0.003) that gross state product does not, so 49 contiguous units carry appreciably less
independent information than their count suggests. It is sampled, and the release does not currently carry uncertainty intervals: only 14% of adjacent state rankings
are separable at the 95% level, and the middle boundary of the four-tier classification distributed
with the data is resolved in 47.4% of resamples.

The occupational layer classifies only 27.4% of usage in the median state, restricting a shift-share
decomposition to six of forty-seven jurisdictions. Within that bounded illustration, composition and
intensity point in opposite directions for states with
similar published indices: California's advantage is entirely within-occupation intensity while
Washington's is occupational composition partly offset by below-national intensity.

We further show that a policy estimate keyed to United States state artificial-intelligence statutes
effective 1 January 2026 is not identified in these data, and we report the non-identification rather
than an estimate. We close with a disclosure standard for measurements used in high-consequence
decisions.


**Keywords:** threshold instability; modifiable areal unit problem; specification uncertainty;
measurement error; AI adoption; index construction; evidence disclosure

## 1. Introduction

Measurements of where artificial intelligence is being adopted have moved rapidly from industry
reporting into policy argument. Legislative testimony, regulatory impact assessments, and
international development discussion now routinely cite figures describing which regions use these
systems more or less than expected. The figures are produced quickly, from proprietary telemetry, and
are typically published as ordered lists of places.

The dominant construction is a representation index. For unit *i*, with observed usage
\(u_i\) and a population base \(p_i\),

$$ R_i \;=\; \frac{u_i / \sum_j u_j}{\,p_i / \sum_j p_j\,} $$

so that \(R_i = 1\) denotes proportional representation. This object is not new. It is the location
quotient of regional economics (Isard, 1960), the parity ratio of the conservation representation
literature (Scott et al., 2001), and structurally a ratio of two compositions in the sense of
Aitchison (1986). Its statistical properties are therefore known, but they are known in literatures
that the AI measurement discussion has not yet drawn on.

The composite indicator literature has developed an extensive apparatus for exactly this problem.
Saisana et al. (2005) set out uncertainty and sensitivity analysis as a requirement rather than an
option for indices used in policy; Paruolo et al. (2013) show that nominal and effective indicator
weights routinely diverge; and Kuc-Czarnecka et al. (2020) demonstrate, using the World Bank's Doing
Business Index, that a composite can be reconstructed to support more than one narrative without
changing the underlying data. None of that apparatus has been applied to a published AI adoption
statistic.

Three properties matter and are, to our knowledge, unaddressed in published AI adoption statistics.

First, the index is areal. It is computed on administrative units whose number and boundaries are
analyst choices. The modifiable areal unit problem (Gehlke and Biehl, 1934; Openshaw, 1984;
Fotheringham and Wong, 1991) establishes that statistics computed on areal aggregates change with
both the *scale* of aggregation and the *zoning* of boundaries at fixed scale. The consequences for a
representation index have not been quantified for any AI adoption measure.

Second, usage shares are compositional. Usage shares sum to unity by construction, so a genuine increase in
one unit mechanically depresses every other unit's index. This violates the independence assumptions
of standard regression on such outcomes (Aitchison, 1986) and, in a policy-evaluation setting,
violates the stable unit treatment value assumption.

Third, the published values are estimates from finite samples. Published figures derive from finite windows of conversation data. Cells
below a disclosure threshold are suppressed. Yet no published AI adoption index we are aware of
reports uncertainty intervals, while all present results as ordered rankings, a format that invites
readers to interpret small differences in position.

We take the Anthropic Economic Index (Handa et al., 2025) as our case. We selected it because it is released
openly enough to permit external reconstruction, which is what makes this analysis possible at all.
The properties we describe follow from the construction and are not specific to this publisher. We use five geographic waves spanning August 2025 to May 2026,
covering 51 United States jurisdictions.

Our concern is distinct from the substantive questions this dataset is usually used to answer, such
as which occupations are exposed or how adoption relates to national income. We take no position on
those. We ask instead what the geographic construction of the index can support, a question that the
composite indicator literature has long treated as prior to interpretation (Saisana et al., 2005;
Paruolo et al., 2013).

Our contribution is fourfold. We quantify the scale and zoning sensitivity of a live AI adoption
index against a spatially appropriate null. We establish the effective number of independent
observations the index supports. We test whether the publisher's own tier classification survives
sampling uncertainty. And we document a case in which a plausible policy question is not identified
in these data, and argue that reporting the non-identification is the more useful result.

## 2. Data

### 2.1 Source

The Anthropic Economic Index publishes aggregated, privacy-preserving statistics derived from
Claude.ai conversations, classified to occupational tasks using the Clio system (Tamkin et al., 2024)
and the O\*NET task taxonomy. Geographic breakdowns are released under a Creative Commons Attribution
licence. Reports and underlying data are documented in Handa et al. (2025) and subsequent releases.

We use every release carrying United States subnational geography:

| Wave | Sample window | Cadence | Product universe | Raw counts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2025-08 | 4 to 11 Aug 2025 | week | Free and Pro | yes |
| 2025-11 | 13 to 20 Nov 2025 | week | Free and Pro | yes |
| 2026-02 | 5 to 12 Feb 2026 | week | Free, Pro and Max | yes |
| 2026-04 | April 2026 | month | not labelled in file | no |
| 2026-05 | May 2026 | month | not labelled in file | no |

**Table 1.** Release characteristics. Two features constrain everything downstream: the product
universe expands between the November 2025 and February 2026 waves, and the two 2026-06-release waves
report shares only, without the conversation counts required for resampling.

### 2.2 Harmonization

The four source releases use four incompatible schemas. Geography is keyed as `state_us` with bare
two-letter codes in the first release, as `country-state` with `US-` prefixes in the next two, and as
`subregion` in the fourth, which also renames the entire metric vocabulary. We wrote a per-release
parser normalizing all four into a single state-by-wave panel of 255 observations across 51
jurisdictions (50 states and the District of Columbia).

The final release omits conversation counts. Because the index is defined as a ratio of shares, the
implied population share is recoverable as \(p_i = u_i / R_i\); recovered shares sum to 100.000 and
99.977 in the two monthly waves. We hold the working-age population published with the August 2025
release fixed across all waves. This is deliberate: a fixed denominator isolates change in usage from
change in population re-estimation. Our recomputed index tracks the published index to a median
absolute deviation of 0.002.

Holding the denominator fixed is a choice, so we test it. For the three waves in which a
contemporaneous denominator is recoverable, the fixed and contemporaneous versions of the index
correlate at r = 1.0000, 0.9998 and 0.9996 in logs, and the resulting Gini coefficients differ by at
most 0.0005. No result in this paper depends on the choice.

### 2.3 Validation

Because this paper reports errors in a published data product, we subjected our own pipeline to
automated checks before analysis: no duplicate keys, balance across waves, positivity, exact
reproduction of the index by two algebraically independent routes, conservation of totals under
aggregation, and complete coverage of every geographic crosswalk. All hard checks pass. Analysis
predictions were recorded in a version-controlled pre-analysis note before any real data were loaded.

Two errors were caught by these checks and are reported here because their character is instructive.
First, a recovered population share appeared to drift 8.1% between consecutive monthly waves for South
Dakota, suggesting denominator instability; the effect is fully accounted for by two-decimal rounding
in the published shares, which for the smallest states implies ±7% relative precision. Second, an
incomplete crosswalk left two non-contiguous states unmapped to time zones, and because grouped
aggregation drops missing keys silently, one geography was briefly computed on 49 units while being
compared against others computed on 51.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Decomposing the effect of scale

We use the Theil index (Theil, 1967), the member of the generalized entropy family that decomposes
exactly into between-group and within-group components (Shorrocks, 1980):

$$ T \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_g \pi_g \frac{\mu_g}{\mu}\ln\frac{\mu_g}{\mu}}_{T_{\text{between}}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\sum_g \pi_g \frac{\mu_g}{\mu} T_g}_{T_{\text{within}}} $$

with \(\pi_g\) the population weight of group *g*. The quantity \(T_{\text{between}}/T\) is the share
of total inequality that survives aggregation to that grouping, and \(1 - T_{\text{between}}/T\) is
the share destroyed. Unlike the Gini coefficient, this decomposition is exact, not approximate;
our maximum observed residual is 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁶.

We report the Gini coefficient alongside for interpretability, but not as a headline statistic at
fewer than eight units, where four to seven observations make it unstable.

### 3.2 A spatially appropriate zoning null

To separate the *zoning* effect from the *scale* effect we hold the number of units fixed and vary
boundaries. Two comparators are used.

First, five real United States government geographies: Census regions, time zones, Bureau of Economic
Analysis regions, Census divisions, and the ten-region system shared by EPA, HHS and FEMA. These are
not hypothetical partitions; two agencies within the Department of Commerce publish different maps of
the same country at *k* = 8 and *k* = 9.

Second, a Monte Carlo null of random contiguous partitions, generated by multi-source region
growing on the rook-contiguity graph of the lower 48 states and the District of Columbia. Contiguity
is not cosmetic. Because usage is spatially autocorrelated (Section 4.3), a null of arbitrary
non-contiguous partitions would understate what a plausible administrative map achieves, and would
bias the observed map toward appearing exceptional.

The contiguity requirement excludes Alaska and Hawaii. We therefore report the official-map
comparison on all 51 jurisdictions and the null comparison on the contiguous 49, and state which
throughout. This is why time zones appear as *k* = 6 in the first comparison and *k* = 4 in the
second.

### 3.3 Sampling uncertainty and rank resolution

For waves publishing conversation counts, we treat the vector of state counts as a single multinomial
draw and resample 4,000 times, propagating each draw through the index. For each ordered pair we
compute \(P(R_i > R_j)\) and call the pair *resolved* when that probability exceeds 0.975 or falls
below 0.025.

We apply the same procedure to the four-tier `usage_tier` classification distributed with the data,
asking for each adjacent tier pair how often every member of the higher tier exceeds every member of
the lower. This tests a classification the publisher has already made rather than one we impose.

For the count-free waves we substitute a rounding-interval Monte Carlo, drawing uniformly within the
interval implied by two-decimal reporting. We label this a lower bound on total uncertainty, since
sampling error is unobservable without counts.

### 3.4 Spatial structure

We compute Moran's *I* (Moran, 1950) on log-transformed indices using a row-standardized rook
contiguity matrix, with inference by 4,999 permutations.

To convey the practical consequence of that clustering we report a first-order deflation of the
nominal sample size, \(n_{\text{eff}} = n(1-I)/(1+I)\). We state plainly that this is an
approximation and not an exact effective sample size. Exact computation in the sense of Clifford et
al. (1989) and Griffith (2005) requires the full covariance structure of the process, which we do not
estimate. The figure should be read as an order-of-magnitude indication that state-level observations
carry less independent information than their count suggests, not as a precise degrees-of-freedom
correction.

### 3.5 Composition versus intensity

To ask *why* a state's index is high we decompose it into occupational composition and
within-occupation intensity. With \(e_{so}\) the employment share of occupation *o* in state *s*
(Bureau of Labor Statistics OEWS), \(r_{so}\) usage per worker, and \(r_o\) the national rate:

$$ \underbrace{R_s - R_n}_{\text{total}} \;=\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_o e_{so} r_o - R_n}_{\text{composition}} \;+\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_o e_{so}(r_{so} - r_o)}_{\text{intensity}} $$

The identity is exact by construction and is enforced computationally. This is the shift-share logic
of Bartik (1991) and Bound and Holzer (2000), applied descriptively, not as an instrument; we
make no causal claim and the concerns raised by Goldsmith-Pinkham et al. (2020) about Bartik
instruments do not apply to a descriptive decomposition.

### 3.6 Policy estimation and its limits

We specified a difference-in-differences design on United States state artificial-intelligence
statutes effective 1 January 2026, with treatment intensity defined as the population share of a unit
living under such a statute, and a pre-specified grid of 36 defensible specifications across
geographic scale, denominator, weighting, and outlier inclusion, in the manner of Simonsohn et al.
(2020). Section 4.6 reports why we do not present an estimate.

### 3.7 Threshold analysis

We apply the rule "unit index > 1.0", chosen because it requires no external calibration, and vary
only the analytical configuration. Two grids are used. The state-level grid varies wave and outlier
inclusion, holding geography fixed. The regional grid varies the official geography and the wave,
assigning each state the classification of the region containing it. Shares are renormalised after any
unit is removed, which is the correct operation on compositional data. For each unit we report the
crossing rate, the proportion of configurations placing it above the threshold, and treat a unit as
stably classified when that rate exceeds 95% or falls below 5%.

We separately decompose uncertainty in the headline statistic into a sampling component, the standard
deviation of the Gini across 3,000 multinomial resamples at fixed geography, and a specification
component, the standard deviation across the five official geographies at fixed data. The ratio is the
quantity of interest, following the logic of specification curve analysis (Simonsohn et al., 2020).

### 3.8 Reproducibility

All analysis is in Python. Code, the harmonized panel, the pre-analysis note, and an automated test
suite are available at https://github.com/vkenned2/aei-index-audit. All stochastic procedures use a single fixed seed.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Aggregation destroys most measured inequality

| Grouping | *k* | \(T_{\text{between}}/T\) range | Inequality destroyed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Census region | 4 | 0.402 to 0.481 | 52 to 60% |
| Time zone | 6 | 0.241 to 0.476 | 52 to 76% |
| BEA region | 8 | 0.495 to 0.579 | 42 to 51% |
| Census division | 9 | 0.521 to 0.590 | 41 to 48% |
| Federal region | 10 | 0.487 to 0.597 | 40 to 51% |

**Table 2.** Theil decomposition across five waves, population-weighted. Maximum decomposition
residual 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁶.

A reader who encounters AI adoption reported by Census division is seeing, at most, 59% of the
variation present at state level. The figure is stable across waves, which is the strongest form of
this result: it does not depend on which release is examined.

The magnitude is weighting-dependent and must be reported as such. Weighting states equally rather
than by population, a claim about *states* rather than about *people*, gives 66 to 74% destroyed;
population weighting gives 41 to 48%; usage weighting gives 43 to 56%. Across all wave-by-convention cells
the range is 41.0% to 74.1%. Any single number quoted without its convention is underspecified.

**Table 3.** Gini coefficient of the usage index under five official United States geographies, all

The spread is not monotonic in *k*. In three of five waves the ten-region federal map yields a

signature that this is a zoning effect distinct from scale: were scale the only operative mechanism,
the ordering would follow *k*.

### 4.3 The reporting map sits near the top of its null distribution

| Wave | Observed (Census div.) | Null median | Null range | Percentile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2025-08 | 0.2500 | 0.2344 | 0.105 to 0.364 | 81 |
| 2025-11 | 0.2109 | 0.1949 | 0.089 to 0.341 | 78 |
| 2026-02 | 0.1693 | 0.1469 | 0.055 to 0.289 | 95 |
| 2026-04 | 0.1615 | 0.1405 | 0.062 to 0.284 | 95 |
| 2026-05 | 0.1730 | 0.1510 | 0.065 to 0.293 | 95 |

**Table 4.** Observed Census-division Gini against 2,000 random contiguous nine-region partitions of
the contiguous 49 jurisdictions.

The observed value never falls near the median of its null. In the three most recent waves it sits at
the 95th percentile, meaning that 95% of the contiguous nine-region partitions generated by our
sampler report less regional inequality.

We do not read this as evidence that the map is arbitrary. Census divisions were constructed to group
states by economic and settlement similarity, so it is expected that they separate high from low usage
better than chance. The finding is the *magnitude*: a regional inequality figure computed on Census
divisions sits high in the range produced by alternative contiguous partitions of the same data. We
describe percentiles with respect to the ensemble our sampler generates. Multi-source region growing
does not sample uniformly from the space of all contiguous partitions, so these are percentiles
within a generated ensemble and not within the full space of plausible administrative maps.

Extending the null across resolutions qualifies this. At the February 2026 wave, real maps fall at the
64.5th percentile (Census regions, *k*=4), 76.7th (time zones, *k*=4), 81.0th (BEA, *k*=8), 95.6th
(Census divisions, *k*=9) and 65.2nd (federal regions, *k*=10). Every official geography exceeds the
median of its null, which is itself a finding, administrative boundaries are systematically more
division-revealing than random ones, but the extreme value is specific to the nine-region Census map.

The null spread also grows sharply as maps coarsen: the range of achievable Gini values is 442% of
the null median at *k* = 4 against 113% at *k* = 10. Boundary dependence is greatest for the coarsest partitions.

### 4.4 Spatial structure reduces the independent information in the panel

Log usage index exhibits significant positive spatial autocorrelation across the contiguous 49
jurisdictions (Moran's *I* = 0.278, permutation *p* = 0.003). Gross state product per working-age
capita does not (*I* = 0.139, *p* = 0.11), nor does the automation share of conversations (*I* =
0.121, *p* = 0.16). AI usage is more spatially structured than the economic variable most often
invoked to explain it.

A first-order deflation of the nominal sample size gives roughly 28 effective observations
against 49, indicating that inference treating states as independent is overconfident. As noted in
Section 3.4, this is an approximation rather than an exact effective sample size, and we report it
only to convey magnitude.

### 4.5 Rank and tier resolution under sampling uncertainty

In the February 2026 wave (222,372 United States conversations across 51 jurisdictions), multinomial
resampling resolves 14% of adjacent rankings at the 95% level. Across all pairs, 89% are resolved;
the information in the index is in its broad strata, not in fine ordering.

The publisher already coarsens: a four-tier `usage_tier` variable, of roughly 13 jurisdictions each,
is distributed with the data. Applying the same resampling to those tiers:

| Boundary | P(strict separation) |
|---|---|
| Tier 1 vs 2 | 0.700 |
| **Tier 2 vs 3** | **0.474** |
| Tier 3 vs 4 | 1.000 |

**Table 5.** Probability that every member of the higher tier exceeds every member of the lower,
across 4,000 resamples.

The upper boundary is fully resolved. The middle boundary, the cut between the second and third
quartiles of usage, is resolved in fewer than half of resamples, which suggests a three-tier
presentation would be better supported by the sampling design than a four-tier one.

### 4.6 A policy estimate that is not identified

Two independent features of these data preclude a difference-in-differences estimate keyed to
1 January 2026.

The product universe expands from "Free and Pro" to "Free, Pro and Max" between the November 2025 and
February 2026 waves, which is precisely the treatment boundary. Any pre-post contrast confounds
statutory change with a change in the population of conversations observed.

More fundamentally, ordinary between-wave movement exceeds any plausible effect. Total variation
distance between consecutive waves is 12.56, 6.98, 2.88 and 1.48 percentage points respectively. The
largest is the August-to-November boundary, which carries no platform change and no policy
change. Scaled against multinomial sampling error, the median state's August-to-November share change
is 3.4 standard errors, with several exceeding 40. Wave-to-wave variation is therefore dominated by
something other than sampling, and it swamps the effect a state deployer statute could plausibly
produce on consumer usage.

We therefore report no coefficient. The August-to-November transition provides an empirical benchmark
for ordinary between-wave instability: 12.6% of
national usage share changes hands between two ordinary consecutive releases with no identifiable
external event. Movement smaller than that is not interpretable, and we are not aware of any published
benchmark of this kind for an AI adoption index.

One pattern in the series warrants documentation without explanation. Between the August and November 2025
waves, California's share of United States usage falls 7.69 percentage points and New York's rises
6.48, while the remaining 47 jurisdictions move 1.21 points combined; the February wave largely
reverses both. The correlation between first-period and second-period share changes is −0.665 across
all states but +0.152 with these two excluded. Published shares reconcile exactly with published
counts, the national total is stable or growing across the three waves (208,198; 219,666; 223,004
conversations), and the unclassified-geography bucket is empty. We can eliminate parsing error, volume shock, and reattribution to an unclassified bucket. A change
in geolocation or attribution methodology between releases would be consistent with the pattern, but
we have no information bearing on that and do not speculate further. We note it because any panel
analysis using the November wave inherits it.

### 4.7 Convergent validity

Regressing log index on log gross state product per working-age capita gives a slope of 1.768
(SE 0.312) and *R*² = 0.396 (*n* = 51). Within the United States, income explains under 40% of
variation in AI usage, and residual standard deviation is 0.486 log points, a typical deviation of
63%. Utah is the largest positive residual (+1.67), using AI far more than its income predicts;
Alaska, South Dakota, North Dakota and Wyoming are the largest negative residuals.

The residual remains spatially clustered (Moran's *I* = 0.228, *p* = 0.015). What income cannot
explain is not spatially random.

### 4.8 Composition versus intensity, where it can be measured

The decomposition of Section 3.5 requires the occupational distribution of a state's usage. That
distribution is largely unobserved. In the median state only 27.4% of conversations are classified
to a Standard Occupational Classification major group; the remainder is published as
`not_classified`. Coverage ranges from 65.9% (California, 21 of 22 groups) to 2.6% (Hawaii and
Nebraska, one group each), and 17 of 47 states report three or fewer occupations.

This is consequential and easy to mishandle. Dropping the unclassified residual and rescaling the
remainder to 100%, the natural first instinct, reassigns unclassified conversations to whichever
occupations happen to clear the disclosure threshold. For sparse states this attributes a state's
entire volume to one occupation: our own first implementation did exactly this and returned an index
of 20.1 for Vermont, whose published index is 1.10. We report the error because the arithmetic was
correct throughout and only an external plausibility check exposed it.

We therefore restrict the decomposition to states with at least ten occupational groups and at least
50% of usage classified. Six states qualify (California, New York, Texas, Florida, Virginia,
Washington), together accounting for 54% of United States conversations. Usage is counted as
classified conversations only, so the rate is classified conversations per worker; the resulting index
preserves the ordering of the published index (Spearman correlation 1.00 across the six).

| State | Index | Composition | Intensity | Dominant | Groups |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| California | 1.473 | −0.00004 | **+0.00071** | intensity | 21 |
| Washington | 1.204 | **+0.00086** | −0.00057 | composition | 13 |
| Virginia | 1.064 | **+0.00049** | −0.00040 | composition | 15 |
| New York | 1.031 | −0.00012 | **+0.00016** | intensity | 20 |
| Texas | 0.542 | −0.00004 | **−0.00061** | intensity | 17 |
| Florida | 0.496 | −0.00017 | **−0.00055** | intensity | 17 |

**Table 6.** Shift-share decomposition of deviation from the national classified-usage rate.
Components are in conversations per worker. Identity residual 0.0.

The two highest-usage states are high for opposite reasons. California's occupational composition
predicts essentially no advantage (−0.00004); its entire deviation is within-occupation intensity,
meaning Californians in a given occupation use Claude more than their counterparts elsewhere.
Washington is the reverse: its occupational mix predicts a substantial advantage (+0.00086) which is
partly offset by *below*-national intensity within occupations. Virginia follows Washington's pattern,
New York California's.

Both below-national states are below on intensity rather than mix. Texas and Florida have
occupational compositions close to the national profile; their lower usage is workers in the same
occupations using these systems less.

We emphasise the sample is six states and the classification is content-inferred rather than
employment-based, so a student writing code is counted under Computer and Mathematical occupations.
Both considerations argue against generalising the direction of these results. What does generalise is
that composition and intensity are separable, that they point in opposite directions for states with
similar published indices, and that the published occupational layer supports the separation for six
states out of forty-seven.

### 4.9 Threshold instability

Sections 4.1 to 4.8 describe how the index behaves. Policy does not consume indices; it consumes
decision rules laid on top of them. We therefore apply the simplest such rule, that a unit uses AI
above proportional (index > 1.0), and vary only the analytical configuration.

**State-level classification.** Holding the geography at state level and varying wave and outlier
inclusion gives ten configurations per state. Nine states cross the threshold in every configuration
and thirty in none. Twelve states, 24% of the total, cross in some configurations and not others,
ranging from Maryland at 80% to Delaware, New Hampshire and New Jersey at 20%.

**Regional classification.** The rule "the region containing this state is above proportional" is a
more realistic policy form, because regional designations are how such statistics are usually
reported. We evaluate it two ways: varying the map within a fixed wave, which isolates geography, and
across the full grid of five maps by five waves.

| Source of variation | States changing classification |
|---|---|
| Map only, within 2025-08 | 20 of 51 (39%) |
| Map only, within 2025-11 | 25 of 51 (49%) |
| Map only, within 2026-02 | 18 of 51 (35%) |
| Map only, within 2026-04 | 19 of 51 (37%) |
| Map only, within 2026-05 | 17 of 51 (33%) |
| Wave only, at fixed map (mean over five maps) | 9 of 51 (17%) |
| Full grid, five maps by five waves | 34 of 51 (67%) |

**Table 7.** Regional threshold classification, decomposed by source of variation.

Geography is the larger source but it is not the only one. Within any single wave, a mean of 39% of
states change classification across the five maps. Temporal variation contributes a further amount
that is highly uneven across maps: Census regions and BEA regions produce no classification change
across the five waves, Census divisions produce 16% and federal regions 12%, while the time-zone
partition produces 59%. The combined figure of 67% therefore reflects both sources, and the
map-attributable component is the 39% within-wave mean.

The pattern is not noise concentrated in marginal cases. Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine, New
Jersey, New Hampshire and Vermont are classified above threshold in 100% of BEA-region,
Census-division, Census-region and federal-region configurations, and in 40% of time-zone
configurations. The states are identical, the conversations are identical, and the classification
depends on which federal agency drew the map.

sampling uncertainty.

|---|---|
| Sampling (3,000 multinomial resamples) | 0.00116 |
| Specification (five official maps) | 0.01674 |
| Ratio | **14.4** |

The sampling 95% interval is [0.2116, 0.2161]. The range across official maps is [0.1248, 0.1671].
**The two do not overlap.**

The comparison above varies resolution as well as boundaries, since the five official maps span four
to ten regions. To isolate boundary placement from scale we repeat it at fixed resolution. Among
1,500 random contiguous nine-region partitions, the standard deviation of the Gini is 0.01832 against
a sampling standard deviation of 0.00121 computed at that same resolution, a ratio of 15.2. Computing
the sampling standard deviation within each official configuration rather than at state level gives
0.00114 on average and a ratio of 14.7. The conclusion is insensitive to which of the three
comparisons is used.

The result is not an artefact of the Gini. Repeating the decomposition on the Theil index gives a
sampling standard deviation of 0.00082 against a specification standard deviation of 0.00612, a ratio
of 7.5, with a sampling interval of [0.0756, 0.0787] against a map range of [0.0290, 0.0434]. These
intervals are also disjoint. The ratio is smaller because the Theil index responds differently to the
tail, but the qualitative conclusion is identical under both measures. Any interval a publisher could construct from the sampling design alone
excludes every value the choice of an equally official map would produce.

Published uncertainty measures for adoption indices of this kind generally address sampling
uncertainty. The results above indicate substantial additional variation associated with geographic
specification, which is straightforward to report alongside it.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 What these results do and do not say

These results do not indicate an error in the Anthropic Economic Index. Every areal index has the properties we describe; they follow from the construction, and the modifiable areal unit problem has
been understood for ninety years (Gehlke and Biehl, 1934). Our contribution is to size them for a live
index that is being cited in policy, to show that the resulting instability reaches the decision and
not only the estimate, and to supply tooling that re-runs the checks on each release.

The analysis was possible because the underlying data were released under an open licence. The properties we quantify are structural to this class of measure and are likely present in
proprietary AI adoption statistics that cannot be examined at all.

### 5.2 Implications for how such indices should be published

A measurement that carries a decision rule needs more disclosure than one that does not, because a
reader cannot reconstruct the sensitivity of a classification from a point estimate. We propose that
any measurement used for a high-consequence decision be published with four items.

**The configuration grid considered.** Which analytical choices were varied and over what range. Ours
was five official geographies, three weighting conventions, outlier inclusion, and five waves. A grid
fixed before analysis also constrains the analyst, which is why ours is in a version-controlled
pre-analysis note.

**The crossing rate.** For each unit, the share of configurations under which it falls on each side of
the threshold. This is the quantity a decision-maker actually needs and it is currently reported
nowhere. In our case it converts a clean-looking classification into the statement that a mean of 39%
of states cannot be stably classified within a single wave, and 67% across the full grid.

**The variance decomposition.** Specification standard deviation alongside sampling standard
deviation. Publishing only the latter, as is universal, understates total uncertainty by a factor
that in our case is 14.4.

**The configurations that flip the conclusion.** Naming them is more useful than a summary statistic,
because it tells a reader which choice to argue about. In our case the time-zone geography flips six
New England states that every other official map classifies identically.

Two lower-cost measures follow directly from the results and could be adopted immediately by index
publishers. Report the between-group share alongside any regional figure, so a reader knows how much
variation that scale conceals. And publish intervals, or tiers wide enough to survive them: the
existing four-tier classification is resolved at its upper boundary but not at its middle one.

### 5.3 Generalization beyond one index

Our empirical analysis is limited to one index because it provides the subnational data required for
reconstruction. The construction, however, is generic. Any
statistic of the form "share of observed activity divided by share of a population base, reported by
administrative region" inherits all three properties, and the inheritance is a matter of algebra,
not of any particular publisher's choices. Geographic breakdowns of AI skill penetration,
provider-reported usage statistics, and connectivity-adjusted adoption measures all take this form.

Three of our four diagnostics require only published aggregates and could be applied to any of them
immediately: the scale decomposition needs unit-level values and a hierarchy, the zoning comparison
needs only alternative maps, and the spatial deflation needs a contiguity graph. Only the sampling
analysis requires counts, which most publishers do not release. We regard extending this audit across
indices as the natural next step, and note that the binding constraint is disclosure rather than
method.

The structure generalises past indices altogether. Frontier AI governance is increasingly built on
capability thresholds: a compute level, a benchmark result, an audit trigger. Each is a decision rule
sitting on a measurement, and the evaluation literature has established that benchmark scores are
substantially configuration-dependent. Sclar et al. (2024) report performance differences of up to 76
accuracy points on LLaMA-2-13B from meaning-preserving changes to prompt formatting alone, with
sensitivity persisting as model size, few-shot count and instruction tuning increase, and they
recommend reporting a range across plausible formats in place of a single number. That
recommendation is the same instrument we apply here to geography. That
literature and this paper describe the same problem in different domains. What neither has produced,
and what the disclosure standard above is a first attempt at, is a requirement that the sensitivity of
a threshold conclusion be part of the record alongside the conclusion itself.

We also note an asymmetry worth naming. Because this analysis required open data, it can only be
performed on the indices whose publishers have chosen transparency. The measures most likely to
exhibit these problems unexamined are those that cannot be examined.

### 5.4 Limitations

Our panel spans five waves over ten months, with mixed weekly and monthly cadence; we control for
cadence but cannot eliminate it. Two waves lack conversation counts, so sampling uncertainty there is
bounded by rounding rather than estimated. We fix the population denominator at its August 2025
values, which isolates usage change at the cost of ignoring genuine demographic drift. The zoning null
excludes Alaska and Hawaii by construction.

The threshold analysis uses a single rule, proportional use, chosen because it needs no external
calibration. Real policy thresholds are set by statute or regulation and would produce different
crossing rates; our claim is about the existence and magnitude of configuration dependence, not about
any particular cut point.

Most importantly, the index measures conversations with one provider's consumer products. It is a
measure of Claude usage, not of AI usage, and every result here should be read with that scope.

---

## 6. Conclusion

Indices of AI usage built on geographic shares are sensitive to aggregation, boundary placement,
spatial dependence and sampling variation. In the Anthropic Economic Index these sources are large
enough to change classifications and not only estimates. Holding the wave fixed, a mean of 39% of
states change threshold classification depending on which official United States map is used, rising
to 67% across the full grid of five maps by five waves. The standard deviation of the headline
inequality statistic across geographic configurations is 14 to 15 times its sampling standard
deviation, with the intervals disjoint under all three versions of the comparison we ran, including
one holding resolution fixed so that boundary placement is the only thing varying.

The mechanism is that aggregation to standard geographies removes 41 to 74% of measured inequality
depending on weighting convention, that the map used for regional reporting sits high in the
distribution generated by random contiguous partitions at the same resolution, that usage is
spatially clustered in a way that reduces the independent information in the panel, that only 14% of
adjacent rankings and less than half of one published tier boundary are resolved under resampling,
and that 12.6% of national usage share turns over between ordinary consecutive releases.

These results argue for reporting specification sensitivity alongside sampling uncertainty wherever a
geographic index carries a threshold decision. They also show the value of treating
non-identification as an empirical result. The available panel cannot support a policy estimate keyed
to 1 January 2026 without conflating statutory change with a change in the observed product universe
and with substantial ordinary between-wave instability.
